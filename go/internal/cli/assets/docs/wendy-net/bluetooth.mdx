---
title: Wendy Net over Bluetooth
description: How Bluetooth links work, what they can carry, how fast they are, and how a Bluetooth-only device shows up in mDNS.
---

Bluetooth can reach a robot with no cable, no access point in range and no
Wi-Fi Aware radio. It's the slowest kind of link, so Wendy Net uses it to
carry application traffic when it's the cheapest available path. It can
carry the same types of traffic as every other link.

## Turn it on

Enable Bluetooth on each device that should use it, then check for neighbors:

```sh
wendy net configure --device <device> --participate=true --ble=true
wendy net topology --device <device>
```

Your apps don't need a Bluetooth entitlement for this. The agent manages the
Bluetooth links. [Bluetooth hardware](/docs/hardware/bluetooth#wendy-net-over-bluetooth)
covers radio profiles, transmit power and capacity in more detail.

## How a Bluetooth link works

<Figure src="/images/wendy-net/find-bluetooth.webp" alt="A device sending a row of 31 small cells, under the words Bluetooth, 31 bytes" caption="Each device announces itself with a 31-byte Bluetooth advertisement." />

1. **Each device advertises.** It sends a small Bluetooth Low Energy
   advertisement with tenant and device identifiers. Anyone nearby can read
   the tenant ID.
2. **A neighbor connects.** Data runs over a Bluetooth LE connection-oriented
   channel, a direct stream between the two radios.
3. **Both prove who they are.** Wendy Net doesn't use Bluetooth pairing. Instead,
   the two devices run mutual TLS over the channel with their enrollment
   certificates, exactly as they would over Ethernet, and both must belong to
   your organization. Devices skip sending the certificates the other side
   already has, so the handshake fits a slow link.
4. **The link joins the network.** From then on it's an ordinary Wendy Net link
   with a cost of 4096. Reconnecting to a device it has met before is quicker,
   because the TLS session can resume.

A WendyOS device keeps up to three Bluetooth neighbors at once, and starts at
most two of those links itself. It also keeps one extra slot for a computer
running the Wendy CLI's router.

## What it can carry

A Bluetooth link carries real network traffic. An app can make any HTTP, TCP or
UDP call to an app on another device, even when the only link between them is
Bluetooth, and a Bluetooth link can be one hop of a
[longer route](/docs/wendy-net/multi-hop) that also crosses Ethernet or
Wi-Fi Aware.

<Figure src="/images/wendy-net/any-tcp-call.webp" alt="The command curl 10.99.0.7:18080 returning 200 OK between two devices joined by a dotted Bluetooth line, with the words Any port the app publishes" caption="An ordinary HTTP call to another device's service, over a Bluetooth link." />

It's slow, though. Expect kilobytes per second rather than megabytes, and
delays of hundreds of milliseconds on each Bluetooth hop. That suits commands,
alerts, status updates, sensor readings and small API calls. It doesn't suit
video, large files or container images: move those over Ethernet, Wi-Fi or
Wi-Fi Aware.

Bluetooth buffers only a few kilobytes of app packets, for up to a few seconds.
If an app sends faster than the link can carry, the extra packets are dropped
instead of piling up into minutes of delay. Use TCP, or retry in your app,
for data that must arrive.

## Does a Bluetooth device show up in mDNS?

On the radio, no. Inside your app, yes.

<Frames columns={2}>
  <Frame src="/images/wendy-net/mdns-on-the-radio.webp" alt="A device's mDNS announcement stopping at its own boundary, never reaching a device joined to it by a dotted Bluetooth line, with the words No multicast between devices" caption="On the radio, no: multicast stays on each device." />
  <Frame src="/images/wendy-net/mdns-inside-your-app.webp" alt="An app panel browsing _http._tcp that shows the service 10.99.0.7:18080, joined by a dotted line to a device labelled Bluetooth only" caption="Inside your app, yes: the service appears like any other." />
</Frames>

**On the radio, no.** mDNS works by multicast on one local network, and Wendy
Net doesn't flood multicast between devices. A device that's reachable only
over Bluetooth isn't announced on your LAN, so `dns-sd` or `avahi-browse` on
your laptop won't list it, and its `.local` hostname won't resolve there.

**Inside your app, yes.** Every device's signed manifest lists the services its
apps publish. On each Wendy Net app's private network, WendyOS answers mDNS from
those manifests. So a service on a Bluetooth-only device appears in your app's
ordinary mDNS browse, with an address in `10.99.0.0/16` and the published port,
just like a service on a device across the room on Ethernet. Your app connects
to that address, and Wendy Net carries the connection over Bluetooth.

The service stays listed only while there's a route to its device. When the
Bluetooth link breaks and no other path is left, the service disappears from
your app's browse results. It comes back when the route does.

[Share and discover app services](/docs/guides/wendy-net-services) shows how
to publish and browse a service.

**On your computer, through the CLI.** `wendy discover --type mesh` lists the
devices that the CLI's router can reach through Wendy Net, Bluetooth-only ones
included, named after their hostnames:

```sh
wendy discover --type mesh
```

The CLI starts that router when it needs one, using your saved sign-in. See
[Connect to Wendy Net from your computer](/docs/advanced/development/local-mesh-radio).

## Sharing the radio

- **Apps that use Bluetooth peripherals** still need the `bluetooth`
  entitlement. They share the same adapter with Wendy Net, along with its
  bandwidth.
- **Coded profiles** trade speed for range. They change the scan and connect
  settings for the whole adapter, and both devices must support them. Start
  with the normal profile. See
  [radio profile and transmit power](/docs/hardware/bluetooth#radio-profile-and-transmit-power).
- **The adapter.** The agent uses the first Bluetooth adapter that supports LE
  advertising and turns it on.

## When a link doesn't come up

1. Run `wendy net status` and `wendy net topology` on both devices. Both need
   `--ble=true` and the same organization.
2. Give the first connection time. A first handshake between two devices
   carries their certificates, and over Bluetooth that can take a while.
3. Check the device logs if nothing appears. After a failure the Bluetooth
   carrier restarts about five seconds later, and scanning restarts after
   45 seconds without seeing any Wendy Net advertisement. A powered-off or faulty
   adapter shows up as an error in status.
