---
title: How Wendy Net works
description: How devices get an address, find their neighbors, prove who they are, learn about everyone else and pick a path.
---

This page follows one device from enrollment to its first packet. Start with the
[overview](/docs/wendy-net) if you haven't seen it, and use the
[setup guide](/docs/guides/wendy-net) when you're ready to turn it on.

## One identity, one address

When you enroll a device in your organization, it gets a certificate that names
both the organization and the device. Wendy Net uses that certificate for
everything: to prove who the device is, and to give it its address.

The address is a private IPv6 address made from a fixed Wendy Net prefix,
`fd45:b891:567f::/48`, and the end of the device's ID. Nothing assigns it at
run time, so it never changes, and it is the same on every connection. Whether
a packet arrives over Ethernet or Bluetooth, it is addressed to the same device.

<Figure src="/images/wendy-net/one-address.webp" alt="A device with the example address fd45:b891:567f:4a1c:9e02:77b3:d16f:20a8, above the four connection types Ethernet, Wi-Fi, Wi-Fi Aware and Bluetooth" caption="One certificate gives one address, and every connection uses it. The address shown is an example." />

The device's hostname plays no part in routing. You can rename a device without
changing its address or its paths.

## Finding the neighbors

A device first has to find the devices it can reach directly, its neighbors. It
looks on every kind of connection you have enabled for it:

| Connection | How neighbors find each other |
| --- | --- |
| Ethernet and ordinary Wi-Fi | mDNS on the local network, with the service `_wendy-net._udp`. Links then use QUIC on UDP port 43023. |
| Wi-Fi Aware | The Wi-Fi radios find each other directly, with no access point and no shared network. |
| Bluetooth | Each device sends a small Bluetooth Low Energy advertisement, only 31 bytes long. |

  <Frame src="/images/wendy-net/find-ethernet-wifi.webp" alt="Three devices on one network line, one announcing _wendy-net._udp over mDNS" caption="Ethernet and Wi-Fi: mDNS" />
  <Frame src="/images/wendy-net/find-wifi-aware.webp" alt="Two devices joined by a dashed line, with a crossed-out access point beside them" caption="Wi-Fi Aware: no access point" />
  <Frame src="/images/wendy-net/find-bluetooth.webp" alt="A device sending a row of 31 small cells" caption="Bluetooth: a 31-byte advertisement" />
</Frames>

Every announcement carries the device's identity and its organization, so that
devices can ignore strangers early. On Ethernet and Wi-Fi this information is
visible to anyone on the local network. Enable Wendy Net on networks where that
is acceptable.

## Proving who they are

An announcement is only a hint: anyone can claim to be anyone. So before a link
carries any traffic, both devices present their enrollment certificates to each
other over mutual TLS. Each side checks that the other's certificate is valid
and that it belongs to the same organization. If either check fails, the link
never comes up.

<Figure src="/images/wendy-net/same-organization.webp" alt="Two devices joined by a link, with a third, dashed device below them left out, under the title Same organization" caption="Both devices must prove they belong to your organization. A device from outside is turned away." />

The checks run again on every new connection and every reconnect, so a device
whose certificate has expired can't link again.

## Learning about everyone else

Neighbors are the devices you can reach directly. Everyone else arrives by word
of mouth.

Each device writes a short manifest about itself, with its address, its name and
the app services it offers, and signs it using its enrollment identity.
Neighbors can relay the signed manifest unchanged. Each recipient checks the
signature against the identity of the device that wrote it.

<Frames columns={2}>
  <Frame src="/images/wendy-net/signed-manifest.webp" alt="A card titled Manifest listing Address, Name and Services above a chain of six devices" caption="Each device signs a manifest with its address, name and services." />
  <Frame src="/images/wendy-net/relay-not-forge.webp" alt="A chain of six devices with the words Relay it. Can't forge it." caption="Neighbors pass it along. They can relay it, but not forge it." />
</Frames>

When a link connects or reconnects, WendyOS shares the manifests its neighbor
needs. While the link is up, WendyOS shares new versions as manifests change.
On a busy link, the latest version goes out when there is room.

A device only accepts a route to another device when that device's signed
manifest says the address is really its own. WendyOS renews its own manifest
after five minutes without a new publication. Each revision is valid for at
most fifteen minutes, so a device that disappears ages out on its own.

## Picking a path

Every kind of link has a cost. Faster links cost less:

| Link | Cost |
| --- | ---: |
| Ethernet | 64 |
| Wi-Fi | 128 |
| Wi-Fi Aware | 512 |
| Bluetooth | 4096 |

A route costs the sum of its hops, and traffic takes the cheapest route. Three
fast hops can beat one Bluetooth hop: 64 + 128 + 512 = 704, which is far less
than 4096.

<Figure src="/images/wendy-net/cheapest-path.webp" alt="Two endpoints joined directly by a Bluetooth arc costing 4096, and by three hops below costing 64, 128 and 512, with the sum 704 highlighted" caption="704 beats 4096, so traffic takes the three fast hops." />

When two neighbors share more than one kind of connection, Wendy Net carries
their application traffic on the cheapest one. Ethernet beats Wi-Fi, Wi-Fi
beats Wi-Fi Aware, and Wi-Fi Aware beats Bluetooth. The other connections stay
up as standbys while the radios have room. Standby links still exchange routing
and manifest updates.
[Re-routing and path choice](/docs/wendy-net/rerouting#two-devices-several-connections)
explains what standby links do, and [Multi-hop routing](/docs/wendy-net/multi-hop)
covers longer routes.

## Carrying the traffic

Packets for another device go to the neighbor that is next on the cheapest
route, and each device along the way passes them on. A device never sends
Wendy Net traffic out of its ordinary uplink: if there is no route, the packet
is dropped rather than leaked.

Your apps don't see any of this. They connect to the address of the other app's
service, and the address stays the same while the route changes underneath.

## What your apps see

An app that opts in to Wendy Net gets its own private network inside the device.
On it, the agent answers mDNS for the app services that other devices advertise
in their signed manifests, and gives each one an address in `10.99.0.0/16`. The
app connects to that address with ordinary HTTP, TCP or UDP. The agent carries
the connection to the other device, where only the ports that app published are
reachable.

<Figure src="/images/wendy-net/mdns-inside-your-app.webp" alt="An app panel browsing _http._tcp that shows the service 10.99.0.7:18080, linked by a dotted Bluetooth line to a device labelled Bluetooth only" caption="A service on another device appears in your app's mDNS, even when the only link to it is Bluetooth." />

[Share and discover app services](/docs/guides/wendy-net-services) shows the
`wendy.json` settings and a complete example.

## What Wendy Net doesn't do

- **It doesn't flood multicast between devices.** mDNS traffic stays on each
  device. Apps see other devices' services because the agent answers from the
  signed manifests. See [Wendy Net over Bluetooth](/docs/wendy-net/bluetooth#does-a-bluetooth-device-show-up-in-mdns).
- **It doesn't combine links.** Traffic between two neighbors uses one link at
  a time, and traffic to a destination follows one path at a time.
- **It doesn't link devices across the Internet.** Links form between devices
  that can find each other directly: on the same local network, or within
  Wi-Fi Aware or Bluetooth range.
- **It doesn't give apps Internet access by itself.** Internet access through
  another device is a separate setting. See
  [Reach the Internet through another device](/docs/wendy-net/internet-sharing).
