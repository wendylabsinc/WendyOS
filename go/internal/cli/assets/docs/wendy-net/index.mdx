title: Wendy Net

Wendy Net links your devices into one private network, over whatever connections
they have: Ethernet, ordinary Wi-Fi, Wi-Fi Aware and Bluetooth. Devices pass
traffic along for each other, so a robot can reach a server it has no direct
connection to. Traffic takes the cheapest path, and when a connection drops it
moves to the next best one by itself.

## The idea in five lines

- **One identity, one address.** Each device gets a certificate when you enroll
  it in your organization. That certificate gives it one private address, and
  the address is the same on every connection.
- **Any connection.** Devices find each other on Ethernet and Wi-Fi networks,
  directly over Wi-Fi Aware with no access point, and over Bluetooth.
- **Many hops.** Every device is also a router. If A can reach B and B can reach
  C, then A can reach C, even when each hop uses a different kind of connection.
- **The cheapest path.** Every kind of link has a cost. A route costs the sum of
  its hops, and traffic takes the cheapest route.
- **Only your organization.** Before two devices exchange any traffic, they
  prove who they are with their certificates. A device from another
  organization cannot join.

## Built into WendyOS

Wendy Net is part of WendyOS. The router runs inside the Wendy agent that every
WendyOS image includes, and the image ships with the kernel features it needs.
There is nothing extra to install, and one command turns it on:

```sh
wendy net configure --participate=true --ethernet=true --infrastructure-wifi=true
```

Run it for each device, then check the devices it found with
`wendy net topology`. Add `--ble=true` for Bluetooth, and `--nan=true` for
Wi-Fi Aware on [hardware that supports it](/docs/wendy-net/wifi-aware). The
[setup guide](/docs/guides/wendy-net) walks through every option.

<Figure src="/images/wendy-net/built-into-wendyos.webp" alt="A seafoam block labelled Wendy Net inside a frame labelled WendyOS, under the title Built into WendyOS" caption="Wendy Net ships inside WendyOS. It is off until you turn it on." />

On another Linux distribution, such as Ubuntu with
[Wendy Agent](/docs/installation/linux), the agent runs the same router. There
it's up to you to provide the kernel features it needs, such as IPv6 policy
routing and TUN devices, and drivers for the radios you want to use. These
guides are written for WendyOS.

Wendy Net stays off until you enable it, and you choose which kinds of
connection each device may use. Your apps don't need any radio permissions to
use it: the agent manages the connections, and apps opt in through
[their `wendy.json`](/docs/guides/wendy-net-services).

Your computer can join too. `wendy net connect` starts a router in the Wendy CLI
that uses your organization's sign-in, so you can find and manage devices that
are only reachable through other devices. See
[Connect to Wendy Net from your computer](/docs/advanced/development/local-mesh-radio).

## What your apps get

An app on one device can call an app on another with an ordinary HTTP, TCP or
UDP request, whichever connections sit in between. It finds the other app with
the same mDNS discovery it would use on a local network, and connects to the
address that discovery returns. See
[Share and discover app services](/docs/guides/wendy-net-services).

A device without its own Internet connection can also borrow one from another
device on Wendy Net, several hops away if needed. See
[Reach the Internet through another device](/docs/wendy-net/internet-sharing).
