---
title: Multi-hop routing
description: How traffic crosses several devices, and several kinds of connection, to reach a device out of direct range.
---

Two devices don't need a direct connection to talk over Wendy Net. Every
participating device is also a router: it forwards traffic for the devices
around it. If A can reach B and B can reach C, then A can reach C. Each step
along the way is a hop.

## How a route forms

Each device knows its neighbors, the devices it has a direct link to. Neighbors
regularly tell each other which devices they can reach and how much it costs to
get there. A device adds the cost of its own link to the neighbor and keeps,
for each destination, the neighbor that offers the cheapest total. That
neighbor is the next hop.

So nobody needs a map of the whole network. A only has to know that, for C, the
next hop is B. B knows its own next hop for C, and so on until the packet
arrives.

Routes are tied to identity. A device only accepts a route to C when C's own
[signed manifest](/docs/wendy-net/how-it-works#learning-about-everyone-else)
confirms that the address belongs to C. A device in the middle can pass that
proof along, but it can't make one up, so no device can claim another device's
address.

## Mixed connections on one route

Each hop can use a different kind of connection, and each device only needs to
share one kind with its neighbor on either side. Say A reaches B over Bluetooth,
B reaches C over Ethernet, and C reaches D over Wi-Fi Aware:

<Figure src="/images/wendy-net/three-different-links.webp" alt="Four devices A, B, C and D in a row, joined by Bluetooth, Ethernet and Wi-Fi Aware, with the call fetch http://10.99.0.7 above them" caption="One HTTP call from A to D crosses three hops over three different kinds of connection." />

A and D have nothing in common: A has no Ethernet and D has no Bluetooth. They
can still talk, because B and C bridge the gaps. When an app on A calls an app
on D, your Python, Rust, C or JavaScript code makes one ordinary request to D's
address. Wendy Net carries it across all three hops, and you never touch the
routing.

## Devices on different networks

The same works across networks that have nothing to do with each other. Say a
lab has a wired network, the warehouse next door has its own Wi-Fi network that
isn't connected to the lab's, and a robot in the yard has only Bluetooth.

Wendy Net joins them into one network, as long as a participating device sits
on each boundary with both kinds of connection turned on:

- The **bridge** is plugged into the lab's switch and joined to the warehouse
  Wi-Fi, with Ethernet and Wi-Fi turned on for Wendy Net.
- The **camera** is on the warehouse Wi-Fi and in Bluetooth range of the yard,
  with Wi-Fi and Bluetooth turned on.

```sh
wendy net configure --device <bridge> \
  --participate=true --ethernet=true --infrastructure-wifi=true
wendy net configure --device <camera> \
  --participate=true --infrastructure-wifi=true --ble=true
wendy net configure --device <robot> --participate=true --ble=true
```

Now the lab device can call an app on the robot, three hops away, at a cost of
64 + 128 + 4096 = 4288. There's no new router, VPN or firewall rule between the
two networks: Wendy Net links ride on each network as ordinary local traffic.
Each network only has to allow local mDNS and Wendy Net's peer port, as listed
in the [network requirements](/docs/advanced/wendyos/networking/mdns#network-requirements-and-privacy).

## What a route costs

A route costs the sum of the [link costs](/docs/wendy-net/how-it-works#picking-a-path)
of its hops:

| Route from A to D | Hops | Cost |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Bluetooth, then Ethernet, then Wi-Fi Aware | 3 | 4096 + 64 + 512 = 4672 |
| Ethernet, Wi-Fi, Wi-Fi Aware | 3 | 64 + 128 + 512 = 704 |
| One direct Bluetooth link | 1 | 4096 |

Fewer hops don't always win. In the last two rows, three fast hops cost less
than one Bluetooth link, so traffic takes the three hops. When two routes cost
exactly the same, traffic stays on the one it already uses.

The cost is a guide to speed, not a measurement. A route's real throughput is
set by its slowest hop, and every hop adds a little delay. A route with a
Bluetooth hop in it moves data at Bluetooth speed, however fast the other hops
are.

## Who can read the traffic

The devices in the middle forward packets; they are not the destination. App
connections are encrypted end to end between the two devices, in a session that
checks the destination device's certificate. A device along the way passes the
packets on but can't read them, and it can't pretend to be the device you meant
to reach. Each link is encrypted on its own as well.

Internet traffic that a device borrows through a gateway is different: it isn't
encrypted from end to end by Wendy Net. See
[Reach the Internet through another device](/docs/wendy-net/internet-sharing#who-can-see-the-traffic).

## Practical limits

- **Bluetooth hops are slow.** A route through Bluetooth moves data at
  Bluetooth speed, and setting up a chain of several Bluetooth hops the first
  time can take a while. Prefer Ethernet, Wi-Fi or Wi-Fi Aware for large
  transfers. See [Wendy Net over Bluetooth](/docs/wendy-net/bluetooth).
- **Each radio has a few neighbor slots.** A WendyOS device keeps up to three
  Bluetooth neighbors and four Wi-Fi Aware neighbors at once. It can still relay
  for far more devices than that, because they don't all need to be its
  neighbors.
- **Not every pair on a network links directly.** On Ethernet and Wi-Fi, each
  device starts links to at most two others and accepts links from the rest. On
  a busy network, traffic between two devices can take two cheap hops through a
  third device on the same network.
- **There is no hop limit as such,** but a route can cost at most 65,535. That
  allows up to 15 Bluetooth hops in a row, and far more of the faster kinds.
- **About a thousand devices.** Each device keeps track of up to 1,024 signed
  manifests, its own included, so it can reach about a thousand others.
- **Routes need live neighbors.** A device that drops out takes the routes
  through it with it. See [Re-routing and path choice](/docs/wendy-net/rerouting)
  for what happens next.

## Check a route

`wendy net topology` shows the neighbors and routes as one device sees them.
Check it on the device the traffic starts from, and on the devices along the
way:

```sh
wendy net topology --device <device>
```

One device's view doesn't prove that every other device is reachable. Test the
real request your app makes.
